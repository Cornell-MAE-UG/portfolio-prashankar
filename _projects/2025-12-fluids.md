This project is a dissection of a two-stage rocking piston pump air compressor from the lens of fluid mechanics.
A group of four students and I disassembled a Jun-Air OF332-0B to understand its inner workings.

### Compressor Function
The compressor consists of several key components, namely the inlets and outlets for the low and high-pressure side of the pump, the pistons and piston cylinder, the shaft, the rotary cam, and the cooling fans.
Air enters the low-pressure inlet, is compressed by the first piston, and is sent from the low-pressure outlet to the high-pressure inlet where it is compressed again by the second piston before exiting via the high-pressure outlet.
The vertical movement of the pistons causes rotation of the shaft due to an offset force on the rotary cam.

### Compressor Analysis
The analysis we performed made several key assumptions: the system was modeled as being inviscid, incompressible, quasi-steady, isothermal, and isentropic, with the fans modeled as actuator disks.

The Reynolds number for the flow is calculated using the inlet and outlet values of the flowrate $q$ as specified in the manufacturer specifications ($q_{in} = \mathrm{84 ~L/min}$ and $q_{out} = \mathrm{44 ~L/min}$):

$$
\mathrm{Re} = \frac{\rho U d}{\mu} = \frac{\rho \frac{q}{A} d}{\mu} = \begin{cases} \mathrm{Re_{in}} &= 17,778 \\ \mathrm{Re_{out}} &= 94,574 \end{cases}
$$

For this dissection, I primarily worked on recording measurements, researching compressor specifications, and calculating the volumetric flowrate over the motor from the cooling fans.
This flow rate is critical as it dictates how much heat can be dissipated from the motor.

We calculated the force generated by the disk using the formula for static thrust of a propeller from NASA Report 447:

$$T_0=6000\left(18.7-9.5 \frac{P}{D}\right) \frac{\text{brake HP}}{\mathrm{RPM} \times D}$$

Using the definition of the pitch ($P=0.75 \pi D \tan \beta=17.55 \mathrm{~in}$) and the pitch angle ($\beta$), we calculated $T_0$ to be $8.34 \mathrm{~lbf}$ ($37.10 \mathrm{~N}$).

The actuator disk model allows us to assume that the far downstream velocity, $U_4$, is twice the rotor velocity, $U_3$, and that the force is equal to the mass flow rate $\dot{m}$ multiplied by $U_4$.
Combining this with the definition of mass flow rate, we computed the rotor velocity.

$$U_3=\sqrt{\frac{T_0}{2 \rho A_d}} \impliedby \begin{cases} T_0 & =\dot{m} U_4 \\ \dot{m} & =\rho A_d U_3 \\ U_4 & =2 U_3 \end{cases}  $$


Assuming standard conditions, the volumetric flow rate $Q=A_\mathrm{fan} U_3$ was determined to be $0.403 \mathrm{~m^3/s}$.
For a fan rotating at $1650 \mathrm{~RPM}$, this result is consistent with physical expectations.
